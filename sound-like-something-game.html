How to play

1 Listen Play the short voice clip and pay attention to tone, stress, and pauses.
2 Read the meaning Choose what the speaker meant—not just what the words literally say.
3 See what text loses Compare your interpretation with what a transcript-only assistant might misunderstand.

Why this game

What prosody teaches us about conversational interfaces

I came to HCI from neuroscience, and I wanted this game to make that connection visible. Human communication depends on prosody—the tone, rhythm, stress, and pauses layered over spoken words. Our brains use those signals to infer emotion, intent, emphasis, and even sentence structure.

That matters in HCI because many voice and conversational systems reduce speech to a transcript before deciding what the user meant. The words may survive, while sarcasm, uncertainty, question intonation, contrastive stress, or a meaningful pause disappears. A system can recognize every word correctly and still misunderstand the message.

I made this a listening game because prosody is easier to understand by experiencing it. You hear a short phrase, interpret it almost automatically, and then see how the same words can mislead an interface that only receives plain text.

The four examples explore different signals: sarcasm through tone, a question through rising intonation, meaning created by a pause, and implication created by stressing one word. Together, they show why conversational design needs more than accurate transcription—it needs context, uncertainty awareness, and clarification when meaning is ambiguous.

This is a playful demonstration, not a hearing test or scientific assessment. It is one example of how I connect neuroscience and HCI; the other games explore different ideas from perception and cognition through interaction.
