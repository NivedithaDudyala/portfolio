2. Project Snapshot

Problem

Climate games assume universal values. Generic messaging about polar bears and carbon footprints ignores communities whose environmental ethics are rooted in faith, ritual, and sacred place. Shloka asked whether climate action could feel more meaningful when connected to values players already held.

Research → Design

Led the research-to-design process for a climate game rooted in authentic Hindu practice. Through Tirumala fieldwork, analysis of 412 photos and 43 videos, scholar co-design, and 7 ritual-based game levels, Shloka translated real rituals into gameplay using computer vision, NLP, and audio recognition.

Key Insight

When climate action is framed as sacred duty rather than civic obligation, engagement transforms. Players didn’t adopt new values — they connected environmental protection to beliefs they already held. Authenticity drove this. Aesthetics didn’t.

Outcome

Shloka outperformed 9 existing climate games across immersion, guidance, and climate concern in a controlled 24-participant study. It received ACM DIS 2025 Best Paper recognition, expanded into 5 temple pilots, and was adopted by 2 university courses.

3. My Role

What I owned across research, design, and evaluation

Ethnographic Research & Synthesis

Conducted on-site research at Tirumala and analyzed multi-site data from Tirumala and Sabarimala, coding 412 photos and 43 videos to translate ritual practices, climate cues, and sacred-place relationships into design opportunities.

Co-Design with Scholars

Facilitated sessions with temple priests and Hindu scholars, using their feedback to make the game theologically respectful and to drive the key pivot from deity-control to deity-collaboration.

Ritual Interaction Design

Turned real Hindu rituals — mudras, mantras, breathing, inscription, reading, instruments, and aarti — into embodied digital interactions using computer vision, NLP, audio recognition, touch, and mic-based input.

Game & Narrative Design

Mapped the research findings into a seven-level game structure, pairing each climate challenge with a ritual mechanic, deity mentorship, and the learning arc of knowledge → attitude → efficacy → hope.

Comparative Evaluation

Designed the mixed-method study comparing Shloka against 9 existing climate games with 24 participants, measuring immersion, guidance, climate concern, and engagement.

4. The Problem

A billion people already care about the environment — games ignore why

Most climate games ask players to care through generic environmental messaging: melting ice caps, carbon footprints, endangered animals, and global statistics. Those frames work for some audiences, but they miss communities whose environmental ethics are shaped by faith, ritual, and sacred geography.

In Hindu contexts, rivers can be goddesses, forests can be divine homes, and environmental harm can interrupt pilgrimage, worship, and spiritual fulfillment. Shloka asked whether a climate game could move beyond surface-level representation and use the underlying structure of religious practice as the interaction model.

Research Questions

RQ1 · Narrative Connections

What connects religious stakeholders to climate change, and how can those connections become metaphors in a serious game?

RQ2 · Ritual as Mechanic

How can real rituals be digitized and integrated as game mechanics without flattening or misrepresenting their meaning?

7. Ethnographic Discovery

What we learned

The fieldwork revealed that climate meaning was not separate from religious life. At the temple sites, environmental cues, ritual practices, infrastructure, and sacred geography were deeply connected. These findings shaped how Shloka moved from a climate education concept into a ritual-based game system.

Nature as Living Deities

People did not relate to rivers, trees, and forests as neutral resources. They related to them as sacred beings or divine homes. Design decision: Natural elements became characters with agency, not meters to manage.

Design Decision

Natural elements became characters with agency, not objects or meters to manage. Players interact with nature as something sacred and alive, not as a statistic.

Climate as Religious Crisis

Rainfall disruption, deforestation, polluted rivers, and altered seasonal patterns were not experienced as abstract climate issues. They interrupted pilgrimage, worship, and access to the divine.

Design Decision

Climate harms became barriers to spiritual fulfillment — polluted rivers blocking sacred journeys, smog covering holy places, and environmental damage disrupting ritual life.

Environmental Action as Dharma

Temple sustainability practices were framed less as policy and more as religious obligation. Protecting nature was not presented as a civic task; it was tied to duty, devotion, and respect for the divine.

Design Decision

Shloka framed climate action as fulfilling sacred responsibility, not adopting a new environmental value from scratch.

6. Co-Design Pivot

From deity-control to deity-collaboration

Moving from fieldwork insights to game design required more than cultural inspiration. Because Shloka worked with sacred content, the mechanics had to be theologically respectful — not just visually Hindu.

Iterative co-design artifacts showing early gameplay drafts, scholar feedback, and design sketches that shifted Shloka from deity-control mechanics to a ritual-based collaboration model.

Design Evolution

Early Prototype

Early prototypes explored deity-control and power-based climate gameplay, including a later iteration where Shloka used rituals to gain abilities

Stakeholder Feedback

Prototype review with Hindu cultural stakeholders helped evaluate religious sensitivity, deity agency, and climate representation.

Iterative Steps

Scholar feedback pushed the design away from controlling gods and toward non-violent, ritual-led interactions.

Framework Mapping

The God-Player Problem

The first prototype let players role-play Hindu climate deities, gain powers, and fight pollution. From a game design perspective, it made sense: the player had agency, progression, and a clear power loop.

But during scholar review, that model created a theological problem: it made deities controllable by the player.

“Deities are omnipotent and don’t need to ‘level up.’ Allowing players to control gods reduces divine agency — you’re making the divine subservient to player actions.”

Design Iteration · Control → Collaboration

Before · Control

Players role-played gods, wielded divine powers, and leveled up. It was satisfying as game design, but theologically wrong because it made deities subservient to player action

After · Collaboration

Players embodied Shloka, a chosen child. Instead of controlling deities, players performed rituals correctly, and deities chose to respond with guidance or assistance.

This pivot preserved divine agency, respected the devotee-deity relationship, and turned ritual accuracy into the core skill challenge.

Design Lesson

Cultural accuracy was not visual polish. It reshaped the interaction model and made ritual practice the core mechanic.

7. Rituals as Action

How real Hindu rituals became playable climate mechanics

After the co-design pivot, Shloka’s interaction model shifted away from controlling divine power. The core gameplay came from ritual practice itself: chant the phrase, form the mudra, trace the inscription, offer the light.

Each ritual mechanic was mapped to a specific climate challenge, turning the seven-level game into a sequence of embodied climate actions.

L1 · Renewable Energy

Ritual: Chanting

Interaction: Players chant to summon Vayu, whose winds power a windmill and introduce renewable energy.

Tech: Whisper + Sanskrit pronunciation validation

L2 · Waste Segregation

Ritual: Musical instruments

Interaction: Players use devotional sound to cleanse temple grounds of post-prayer waste.

Tech: Audio interaction + composition

L3 · Sacred Rivers

Ritual: Inscription

Interaction: Players trace holy words to navigate polluted rivers.

Tech: Touch tracing + real-time guidance

L4 · River Cleaning

Ritual: Mudras

Interaction: Players form deity-specific hand gestures to summon the Fish God and purify waters.

Tech: Computer vision / GPT-4V validation

L5 · Air Pollution

Ritual: Breathing

Interaction: Players practice pranayama to clear toxic clouds blocking a holy town.

Tech: Mic-based inhalation and exhalation tracking

L6 · Forest Fires

Ritual: Arati

Interaction: Players use the phone flashlight in circular motion to offer arati and summon Surya.

Tech: Light detection + circular-motion tracking

L7 · Climate Resilience

Ritual: Holy text reading

Interaction: Players read from the Gita to invoke Indra and extinguish fires.

Tech: Whisper + text comparison

Design Principle

Authenticity lived in the mechanic. Shloka was not a climate game decorated with Hindu symbols — its core interactions came from real ritual practice.

8. Evaluation

Testing cultural integration, not just usability

The evaluation showed that Shloka did more than increase engagement. It changed how players understood climate action. Across interviews, participants described a repeated progression: they first reframed climate responsibility as sacred, then felt the harm through familiar holy places, and finally identified concrete actions they could take.

Study Setup

24 Hindu practitioners · 10 games compared · 2+ hour sessions · Surveys + interviews

Immersion

Shloka produced 87% higher immersion than the next-best climate game.

Guidance

84% of participants preferred deity mentors over standard tutorial systems.

Climate Concern

Participants reported the strongest stated intention to take environmental action after playing Shloka.

What the data revealed

The pattern was not just “players liked the game.” Shloka worked by helping players reframe climate action as sacred, feel the harm personally, and identify specific actions they could take.

12. What the Data Revealed

Ritual mechanics increased immersion

Embodied actions made the climate story feel more participatory and memorable.

Cultural familiarity improved clarity

Players understood unfamiliar climate concepts faster when paired with familiar ritual patterns.

Reflection mattered

The most meaningful moments came when gameplay slowed down and asked players to connect action with responsibility.

13. Design Principles

Honor before translating

Study cultural context before turning practices into mechanics.

Make action embodied

Use gesture, sound, rhythm, and pacing to make meaning felt.

Let symbolism carry feedback

Design progress through meaningful signs, not just points.

Balance play with respect

Keep interaction playful without making sacred practices feel trivial.

15. Reflection

This project taught me that cultural interaction design is not about borrowing symbols. It is about understanding the systems of meaning behind actions, then designing experiences that preserve that meaning while opening new ways to participate.

Biggest lesson: meaningful play can help people care, but only when the design respects the world it comes from.
