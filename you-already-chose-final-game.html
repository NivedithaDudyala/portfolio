How to play

1 Choose naturally Pick the option that feels right without overthinking the screen.
2 Inspect the design See the visual and psychological cues that were shaping your attention.
3 Compare View the same information again after the pressure and emphasis are removed.

Why this game

How interfaces influence decisions before we notice

I came to HCI from neuroscience, and I wanted this game to show how attention, context, and mental shortcuts shape everyday choices. People rarely compare every option from scratch. We use cues such as what stands out, what others appear to prefer, what feels scarce, and what a number is compared against.

Those shortcuts are useful because they make decisions faster. In an interface, however, the same mechanisms can be used to guide someone toward a particular plan, purchase, or fare. Visual hierarchy can direct attention, framing can make one option feel safer, an anchor can change how a price feels, and scarcity can shorten the time someone spends evaluating alternatives.

I made this game as a before-and-after experience. First, you choose from a screen designed to favor one option. Then the interface reveals each influence and presents the same core choices more evenly. The contrast makes the design intervention visible without pretending that people make decisions independently of presentation.

The HCI question is not whether an interface influences behavior—it inevitably affects what people notice and understand. The important question is whether that influence supports the user’s goals or primarily serves the business at the user’s expense. Persuasive design becomes deceptive when it hides important information, manufactures pressure, or makes refusal unnecessarily difficult.

The plans, prices, popularity percentages, and scarcity messages in this demonstration are fictional and intentionally constructed. This is one example of how I connect neuroscience and HCI; the other games explore different ideas from perception and cognition through interaction.
